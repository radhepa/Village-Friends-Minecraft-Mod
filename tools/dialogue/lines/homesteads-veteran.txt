## veteran.weather.rain
Rain. Bowstrings go slack and tempers go short. Come in under the arch.
We marched forty days in rain like this once. I didn't see a dry sock till Harrowmere.
Good weather for staying in and oiling leather. Bad weather for everything else.
Rain on stone sounds like a column on the march. I still wake up reaching for my boots.

## veteran.weather.thunder
Thunder. Keep low and stay off the battlements. Lightning likes a tall fool.

Lightning shows you the whole road in one flash. Best scouting you'll ever get, and the shortest.
Thunder doesn't trouble me. It's the quiet between the claps I mind.

## veteran.weather.snow

Snow means tracks. Anything that comes near the tower tonight, I'll know about it by dawn.
The winter at Harrowmere, the snow came to our knees and the bread ran out in a month. This is nothing. Still cold, mind.

## veteran.weather.hot
Hot one. Drink before you're thirsty. A soldier who waits for thirst is a soldier who faints.
In heat like this the old garrison slept in the cellar. I'd join them, if the stairs were kinder.
Too hot for armour. I've hung mine on the target. It wears it better than I do.

## veteran.chat
This tower was built to watch a border. The border moved, or stopped mattering, or both. I'm still here.
Nobody ordered me to stay. That's the funny part. Nobody ordered me to leave, either.
I don't sleep much. Haven't since the war. So I might as well be useful at night.
Pillagers. Banners, crossbows, and not one honest day's work among them. I've no time for them.
I respect anyone who carries a blade properly. And anyone who doesn't need to carry one at all. The second sort are rarer.
Every morning I polish a breastplate I'll never wear again. It's not about the breastplate.
Folk in {village} think I'm daft, keeping watch on nothing. Then something comes over the hill, and they're glad somebody saw it first.
A soldier's first lesson: keep your feet dry. Second: keep your mouth shut. Third: share your bread. I never did learn the second.
The war? Couldn't tell you what it was about. A toll bridge, or a duke's insult. Nobody who fought in it ever knew.
I put new feathers on every arrow each spring. Do they need it? No. Do I? Probably.
The road below is quieter than it used to be. That's either peace or neglect. Hard to tell from up here.
I keep the brazier lit for travellers. If you can see a fire, you know where the walls are.
A good bow's like a good friend. Look after it and it looks after you. Leave it in the rain and it sulks for a week.
Zombies come over the ridge some nights. Slow, stupid and stubborn. Like recruits, except you can't shout them into shape.
Creepers. No battle plan ever written accounts for a bush that hisses at you.
Skeletons can shoot, I'll give them that. Better than half the recruits I ever trained.
Discipline isn't shouting. It's doing the dull thing every day, so that on the bad day your hands know what to do.
My knees forecast the weather better than any shepherd. Left knee for drizzle, right for storms, both for snow.
I count the arrows every evening. It's always the same number. That's a comfort, in a way little else is.
I talk to the practice target sometimes. It's got a lot of holes in its argument.
Merchants come by and ask if I'm the garrison. I tell them I'm the garrison, the cook and the band.
The walls lose a stone here and there. I put back what I can. The tower and I are about even.
Witches are clever. Never fight one in the open; they always have one more bottle than you have patience.
A traveller once asked me what I was guarding. I said, "You, right now." They had no answer to that.
Wolves and I have an understanding. I don't go into their woods at night, and they don't come up my hill by day.
I've seen sunsets over the Greywater and over Harrowmere and over half the border. Best one's still from my own battlements.
Keep your blade oiled, your boots laced and your temper in its scabbard. Three rules. They've served me forty years.
I mend my own boots, darn my own socks and cook my own stew. The army made me useless at everything else, so I got good at those.
The company had a saying: "Lantern lit, nobody lost." I still light one. Old habits.
Some days nothing comes up the road at all. I watch anyway. Quiet's when you watch hardest.
I've buried more good friends than I'd like to count. I've had more good days than that, though. I make sure to count those.
If you carry a shield, carry it like you mean it. Half a shield is no shield.
Peace is a strange thing. You wait your whole life for it, and when it comes you don't know what to do with your hands.
Folk from the village bring up bread now and then. I pay them in arrows. Seems fair to both sides.
I don't hate many things. Pillagers, wet bowstrings and soup with no salt. In that order.
I've kept this watch so long the crows think I'm part of the wall.
Being alone isn't hard. Remembering to talk out loud is hard. Thank you for helping with that.
Young folk ask what war was like. Mostly cold, mostly walking, mostly waiting. Then a few minutes you never forget.
I salute the sunrise every morning. Somebody has to, and the sun outranks me.

## veteran.chat.close
Tamsin. That was the name. My closest friend in the company. We went into the Greywater side by side. Only one of us came out on the far bank.
I had Tamsin's hand in that river. I had it, {player}. The current was stronger than I was. I've been trying to be stronger ever since.

## veteran.past
The Lantern Company. Forty of us at the start, the best and worst soldiers you ever met. We got the name because we never let the camp fire go out.
Captain Varre could hear a pillager sneeze across a valley. Strict as a gate, fair as a scale. Best officer I ever served under.
The siege of Harrowmere lasted a hundred and twelve days. We held the walls. By the end we were boiling boot leather and calling it soup.
Hollis was our cook. Biggest soldier in the company, gentlest hands. Could make a feast out of a turnip and an argument.
Wren taught me the bow. Patient as frost. "Breathe out, then loose," Wren said. I still hear it every time I draw.
Cork was the company joker. Real name Corwin. Told a joke at every roll call, and the captain pretended not to laugh for six years.
Marla was our quartermaster. Counted every arrow, every biscuit, every boot. Lose a spoon, and Marla knew before you did.
The night we crossed the Greywater, the river was in spring flood. The captain said we couldn't wait. The captain was right. It cost us anyway.
At Harrowmere, Cork kept us going with stories. Terrible stories. Every one ended with somebody falling down a well. We laughed every time.
We lost Wren in the last week of the siege. A hundred and five days held, and the best archer I ever knew fell seven days short. I've never forgiven those seven days.
When the war ended, nobody told us for a fortnight. We spent two weeks guarding a bridge for a war that was already over.
Hollis opened a tavern after the war, way down south. Last I heard, the stew was famous and the portions were shameful.
Marla still sends a letter every midwinter. Three lines, always the same: "Alive. Well. Count your arrows." I always do.
Captain Varre's last order was "Go home, Lanterns." Then the captain turned and walked off down the road without looking back. None of us ever saw that face again.
I joined up at sixteen because the recruiting sergeant had a splendid hat. That's the honest truth. I never got a hat.
The captain gave me this bow on the walls of Harrowmere. Said I'd earned it. I've kept it strung and dry every day since.

Night's when I'm most at peace. Strange, for a soldier. But the dark asks less of you.

## veteran.work
Fletching arrows. Goose feathers, three to a shaft. Wren always said two was for amateurs.
Re-pointing the north wall. Mortar and patience. The tower's older than me and in about the same repair.
Polishing the old kit. Breastplate, helmet, buckle. None of it fits any more, but it'll shine.
Drill time. Draw, hold, loose. Draw, hold, loose. Same as forty years ago, only slower.
Carrying wood up for the brazier. Ninety-two steps. I counted them the first day and I've counted them every day since.
Checking the bowstring for fraying. A string that snaps mid-draw will take your eye out. Don't ask how I know.
Patrolling the yard. Yes, it's a small yard. Yes, it still needs patrolling.
Sharpening every blade in the tower. There are eleven. I'm the only one who uses any of them.
Mending the target. I've shot it so many times it's more patch than straw.
Fixing the trapdoor to the battlements. The hinge squeals, and a squealing hinge gives a sentry away.
Writing up the watch log. "Night quiet. One owl. Two foxes. No pillagers." Nobody reads it. I write it anyway.
Sweeping the battlements. Leaves, snow, crow feathers. The tower collects everything but visitors.
Oiling leather. Belts, straps, bracers. Cracked leather fails you at the worst moment, and there's never a good one.
I've been fletching with crow feathers lately. They fly a bit crooked. So do I, these days.
Restacking the woodpile, dry logs on top. If the alarm fire's slow to light, it's no alarm at all.
Walking the perimeter. Counting fence posts, checking for tracks. Finding mostly rabbit.

## veteran.adventure
Heading out? Tell someone where you're going. Then go there. Then come back. That's the whole art of it.
If you're crossing a river in flood, don't. Wait for the water to drop. Whatever's on the far bank will still be there tomorrow. You might not be.
Pillager outposts are tall wooden towers flying banners. Don't go near one alone. Don't go near one with friends either, unless they're very good friends.
Carry more arrows than you think you need, and more bread than that. A hungry squad is a beaten squad.
Out in the wilds, pick your camp before dark, not after. A camp picked in the dark is a camp picked by the enemy.
If you find an old fort out there, test the walls before you trust them. Mine are older than they look, and they look old.
When you're lost, climb. Up high you can see where you've been, and that's half of knowing where you're going.
Strange places out there. Ruins with no names, roads to nowhere. We marched past plenty and never had time to look. You do. Look for me.
Never chase a fleeing enemy into a forest. That isn't them running away. That's an invitation.
Meet a witch's hut in the swamp? Give it a wide berth. I've seen what one bottle does to a whole squad.
Go and see the world, {player}. And when you're tired of it, the brazier will be lit.

## veteran.joke
Hollis's stew was so thick we used it to patch the walls at Harrowmere. Held better than the mortar.
The captain once asked a recruit why the fire had gone out. "Captain, I was watching it the whole time." Best excuse I ever heard. Worst, too.
What's the difference between a pillager and a clod of mud? You can scrape the mud off your boot.
Army breakfast: a biscuit hard enough to stop an arrow. We tested it. Twice.
I asked the quartermaster for new boots. Marla said, "Your feet aren't worn out yet." Couldn't fault the logic.
A skeleton walks into a tavern and orders a drink and a mop. Cork's favourite. Cork never once reached the end without laughing.
Recruits ask me how to stand guard properly. One foot, then the other, then repeat for forty years.
Our drill sergeant was so loud the enemy used to fall in line by mistake.
Why do old soldiers never get lost? We argue with the map until it gives in.
